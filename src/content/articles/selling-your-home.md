# How to sell your house without a realtor in Canada

**Short answer:** to sell your house privately in Canada, hire a real estate lawyer first, research your price, gather your documents, take good photos and advertise the home yourself. You show it to buyers and talk price with them; your lawyer handles the offer paperwork, the deposit, the title transfer and closing. It's legal in every province and territory, and you don't pay a listing commission.

Below are the nine steps, from "should I?" to handing over the keys. They're the same everywhere in Canada outside Québec; where a province does things differently, we say so.

---

## 1. Decide if selling yourself is right for you

*Selling privately works best when the home is ready and you can be there for viewings.*

It tends to go well when:

- Your home is move-in ready, with no big repairs to explain. Good photos do most of the selling.
- You can open the door for viewings on evenings or weekends.
- You have a real estate lawyer, or are happy to hire one.
- You're comfortable answering buyers' questions and talking about price directly.

If you'd rather have someone set your price, negotiate for you and manage the sale, a full-service agent is the better fit. Not sure? Read [Should I sell my house myself?](/guides/should-i-sell-my-house-myself) for the honest pros and cons, and try the [commission calculator](/calculator) to see what's at stake for your home.

---

## 2. Hire your lawyer and call your lender

*Line both up before you list, so you're ready the day an offer arrives.*

In a private sale, your real estate lawyer is the person who protects you. They usually:

- review or prepare the agreement of purchase and sale, and explain each term before you sign
- hold the buyer's deposit in their trust account
- pay off your mortgage from the sale money
- work out the adjustments for property taxes, condo fees and utilities
- transfer the title to the buyer through your province's land registry on closing day

Ask for a fixed-fee quote, and ask whether they've handled private sales without agents. Your provincial law society can help you find one. More in [What a real estate lawyer does when you sell](/guides/what-a-real-estate-lawyer-does-when-you-sell).

**Then call your mortgage lender.** If you have a closed mortgage and sell before the term ends, breaking it usually means a prepayment penalty, which can cost thousands of dollars. Ask for an estimate of the penalty and the discharge fee, and whether you can take the mortgage with you to your next home. That number belongs in your plans before you set a price. More in [Selling a house with a mortgage](/guides/selling-a-house-with-a-mortgage).

> **Province note: British Columbia.** A notary public can handle your sale instead of a lawyer.

> **Ask your lawyer.** Questions for your first call: What documents should I gather? What should I disclose about the home? Will you prepare the agreement, or review one the buyer's lawyer sends? How would you like me to send you offers?

---

## 3. Research your asking price

*Use public information to see what similar homes near you sell for.*

Pricing is your decision. These are the places most sellers start:

- Your latest property assessment from your city or provincial assessment office. It's a useful reference, but it's usually based on an earlier date, so the market may have moved since.
- Recent sales of similar homes nearby: same type, size, age and condition.
- Similar homes listed for sale right now. These are your competition.
- A paid appraisal from a certified appraiser, if you want an independent professional opinion.

Be honest with yourself about condition and location. A home priced close to the market usually gets more viewing requests in its first few weeks. The full method is in [How to research your asking price](/guides/how-to-research-your-asking-price).

---

## 4. Gather your documents and decide what to disclose

*Have the paperwork ready before buyers start asking for it.*

Buyers and their lawyers will ask for some or all of these:

- your latest property tax bill
- your mortgage details, so your lawyer can get a payout statement from the lender
- typical utility and heating costs for a year
- permits and records for renovations, and receipts for major work (roof, furnace, windows)
- a survey or real property report, if you have one
- for a condo: the bylaws, recent meeting minutes, financial statements, reserve fund study and the condo corporation's certificate for buyers

See the full [paperwork checklist](/guides/paperwork-to-sell-your-house-privately), stage by stage.

**Disclosure.** In most provinces, sellers must disclose known hidden defects that make a home dangerous or unfit to live in, such as a leaking basement, structural damage or an old underground oil tank. Some provinces use standard disclosure forms. Hiding a known problem can lead to a lawsuit after the sale.

**Short ownership.** Across Canada, if you've owned the home for less than 365 days, the federal flipping rule may tax your profit as business income unless a life-event exception applies. Talk to an accountant before you list.

> **Province note: British Columbia.** Condo (strata) sellers provide an information certificate called a Form B, and the reserve study is called a depreciation report. BC also has a home flipping tax on profits from homes owned for less than 2 years (730 days), with some exemptions.

> **Province note: Alberta.** Buyers usually expect a Real Property Report (a survey showing the buildings on the lot) with municipal compliance.

> **Province note: Ontario.** Condo buyers usually make their offer conditional on reviewing the condo's status certificate.

> **Ask your lawyer.** What do I need to disclose about my home, and should I use a disclosure statement?

## 5. Get the home ready and take great photos

*Photos are the first viewing. Make them bright, honest and complete.*

- Clean, clear the counters and put away personal items and clutter.
- Fix the small things buyers notice: burnt-out bulbs, dripping taps, sticking doors.
- Shoot in daylight with the lights on and blinds open. Hold your phone level and take photos sideways (landscape).
- Start with the front of the home, then every main room, the kitchen, bathrooms, yard and parking. 10 to 25 photos is plenty.
- Measure rooms or check your measurements. Square footage and room sizes must be accurate.

Describe the home honestly. Mention what's updated and what's original. Buyers trust a listing that doesn't oversell, and an honest listing protects you later. For decluttering, small repairs and staging, see [Getting your home ready to sell](/guides/getting-your-home-ready-to-sell). More tips in [How to list your house for sale by owner](/guides/how-to-list-your-house-for-sale-by-owner).

## 6. Advertise your home

*List it where buyers look, then help it travel.*

On OwnersMarket it takes about 15 minutes:

- Add your photos, price and the basics: rooms, size, taxes and condo fees if any.
- Every listing is reviewed for spam, scams and inappropriate content before it goes live.
- Your exact address stays private until you confirm a viewing.
- You pay a flat $24.99 a month per active listing. No commission, and you can cancel any time.

Then help it travel: put a for-sale sign on the lawn (OwnersMarket gives you a free printable sign with a QR code to your listing), and share the listing link with friends, neighbours and local community groups, following each group's rules.

**Decide now what you'll do if a buyer comes with an agent.** That agent will usually ask you to pay their commission. You can say yes, negotiate an amount, or say no. If you're open to it, tick "Agents welcome" on your listing and say what you'd pay, so agents can find your home and bring their buyers. Whatever you decide, agree it in writing with the agent's brokerage before the viewing, have your lawyer (or notary) look at it, and keep in mind that it reduces what you save. See [Selling privately when the buyer has an agent](/guides/selling-privately-when-buyer-has-an-agent).

## 7. Run safe viewings

*You choose who comes in and when.*

- Accept viewing requests through OwnersMarket, so you have the buyer's name and messages on record.
- Have another adult at home with you, and show one group at a time.
- Put away valuables, keys, medication, mail and documents with your personal details.
- Walk the buyer through the home rather than letting them wander alone.
- Answer questions about the home honestly. For questions about the agreement or the law, say "my lawyer can answer that".

More in [How to show your home safely](/guides/how-to-show-your-home-safely).

> **Warning: watch for scams.** Be wary of a "buyer" who won't view the home, sends more money than agreed and asks for a refund, or asks for your banking or ID details. Never share these. Money only ever moves through the lawyers' trust accounts.

---

## 8. When an offer comes in, your lawyer takes over

*Get legal advice on every offer before you sign it.*

An offer is a written agreement of purchase and sale (called a contract of purchase and sale in some provinces). It usually sets out the price, the deposit, the buyer's conditions (such as financing, a home inspection and reviewing condo documents), and the closing and possession dates.

- Send every offer to your lawyer before you respond.
- You can accept it, make a counter-offer, or decline it.
- The deposit is paid into a lawyer's trust account, as the agreement says. Never take a deposit yourself.

> **Province note: British Columbia.** Conditions are called "subjects", and closing is called "completion". After you accept an offer, the buyer has 3 business days to cancel (the home buyer rescission period). If they cancel, they pay you 0.25% of the price. It applies to private sales too, and neither side can waive it.

> **Province note: Manitoba.** Manitoba's real estate regulation sets out a standard Residential Offer to Purchase Contract that brokerages use. Ask your lawyer whether to use it for your private sale; they'll fill it in or review it with you.

---

## 9. Conditions removed, closing and keys

*Once the conditions are removed, the sale is firm and the lawyers finish it.*

- The buyer removes (or waives) their conditions in writing by the dates in the agreement. After that, the sale is firm.
- Mark your listing as sold and cancel your OwnersMarket plan.
- Your lawyer pays out your mortgage, handles the adjustments and transfers the title on closing day.
- Hand over the keys on possession day, as the agreement says. Leave the home clean and the included items in place.
- Cancel your home insurance and utilities from the possession date, and forward your mail.

At tax time, report the sale on your tax return. The sale of your principal residence has to be reported and designated, even when no tax is owed. An accountant can help.

## Frequently asked questions

**What's the best way to sell a house by owner in Canada?**
Hire your lawyer first, price the home close to recent sales of similar homes, and lead with bright, honest photos. Advertise in more than one place (a for-sale-by-owner site, a lawn sign, your local network) and respond to buyers quickly. Most private sales that stall are priced too high.

**Who prepares the offer in a private sale?**
Usually a lawyer: either the buyer's lawyer drafts it and yours reviews it, or yours prepares it. Some provinces have standard forms. Either way, don't sign anything until your lawyer has gone through it with you.

**How much does it cost to sell a house privately?**
Mostly the same costs you'd have with an agent, minus the listing commission: your lawyer's fee, your mortgage discharge fee and any prepayment penalty, plus moving and any repairs. On top of that you'll pay to advertise, such as $24.99 a month on OwnersMarket. If your buyer has an agent, you may also be asked to pay that agent's commission.

**Do I need a realtor to transfer the title?**
No. Title transfer is legal work done by your lawyer (or notary in BC) through the provincial land registry. A realtor doesn't do it in any sale.

**How do I avoid paying tax when I sell my house?**
If the home was your principal residence for every year you owned it, the gain is generally not taxed under the principal residence exemption, but you still have to report the sale on your tax return. Different rules can apply if you owned it for less than a year, rented it out, or (in BC) owned it for less than two years. Ask an accountant about your situation.

**What if my house doesn't sell privately?**
You can lower the price, improve the photos and description, or switch to a flat-fee MLS listing or an agent. If you sign with an agent, read the listing agreement for any clause about commission on buyers who saw the home earlier.
