# Selling an inherited house in Canada

**Short answer:** when someone dies, their home is usually sold by the **executor** (or administrator) of the estate, not by the person who'll inherit it. In many cases the executor first needs a court **grant of probate** (or administration) before the land registry will transfer the home. For tax, the person who died is treated as having sold the home at market value on the day they died; any increase after that is taxed to the estate or the heir when the home is actually sold. An estate lawyer and an accountant are worth calling early.

> **This is general information, not legal or tax advice.** Estates are a common source of complications. Talk to an estate lawyer about your situation, especially if the estate is complicated or family members disagree.

---

## 1. Who can sell the house?

*Usually the executor, on behalf of the estate.*

- **If the home was owned jointly** (joint tenancy) with someone who survives, it often passes to the survivor outside the estate. The survivor can then sell it in the usual way after the title is updated.
- **If the home was in the deceased's name,** it's part of the estate. The **executor** named in the will (or an **administrator** appointed by the court if there's no will) has the authority to sell it, for the benefit of the beneficiaries.
- **Beneficiaries** don't usually sell the home themselves unless it's transferred to them first.

---

## 2. Probate: do you need it to sell?

*Often, yes, for real estate.*

Probate is the court process that confirms the executor's authority to deal with the estate. For land in the deceased's sole name, the land registry and the buyer's lawyer will usually want to see the grant before the home can be transferred.

- **The rules and fees vary by province.** Your lawyer will tell you what's needed where the home is.
- **Timing matters:** you may be able to list while the application is in progress, but you can't close until the executor has authority to sign the transfer. Build that into your closing date.
- **Minors:** in Alberta, if a minor has an interest in the estate, the Public Trustee must consent to a land transfer, which can take a week or more. Other provinces have similar protections.

---

## 3. Taxes when the house is sold

*Two moments matter: the date of death, and the date of sale.*

- **At death:** the Canada Revenue Agency treats the person who died as having **sold their property at fair market value just before death**. Any gain up to then goes on their final tax return. If the home was their **principal residence**, that gain is usually exempt, but it still has to be designated on the final return.
- **When the estate or heir sells:** if the home sells for more than its value at death, the increase is generally taxable to the estate (on its trust return) or to the heir who sold it. A drop in value may be a loss.
- **If it passes to a surviving spouse or common-law partner,** the tax is generally deferred until the spouse later sells.
- **If an heir moves in** and makes it their own principal residence, different rules can apply to the time they live there.

Get a written **appraisal as of the date of death**: it supports the value on the final return and the gain or loss on the later sale.

> **Ask an accountant** before you sell, especially if the home was a rental or cottage, or if more than one property is involved.

---

## 4. Selling to a family member

*Possible, but fair value and fairness both matter.*

- An executor has a duty to act in the interests of **all** beneficiaries, which usually means selling at fair market value.
- Selling to one beneficiary below value can lead to disputes, and can have tax consequences.
- Get an independent appraisal, put everything in writing, and involve the estate lawyer.

---

## 5. Getting the home ready

*Often the home has been empty for a while.*

- **Insurance:** tell the insurer the owner has died and the home may be vacant; coverage can be limited for unoccupied homes. See [Selling a vacant house](/guides/selling-a-vacant-house).
- **Clear-out:** sort belongings with the family before photos and viewings.
- **Disclosure:** the executor may not know the home's history. Disclose what you know, say what you don't, and talk to the lawyer about how to word it.
- **Price research:** start with the date-of-death appraisal and recent sales nearby; see [How to research your asking price](/guides/how-to-research-your-asking-price).

---

## 6. Selling an estate home privately

An executor can sell privately just like any owner; the estate lawyer handles the legal side as usual. It can keep more money in the estate for the beneficiaries, but the executor carries the work. If the executor lives far away, a local agent may be more practical.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do you have to pay tax when you sell an inherited house in Canada?**
Possibly. The person who died is treated as having sold the home at market value at death (often exempt if it was their principal residence). Any increase after death is generally taxable when the estate or heir sells.

**Do you need probate to sell a house?**
Often, if the home was in the deceased's name alone. If it was owned jointly with a survivor, it may pass outside the estate. A lawyer will confirm what's needed in your province.

**Can an executor sell a house without beneficiaries agreeing?**
An executor has authority to sell estate property, but must act fairly for all beneficiaries and usually at fair market value. If beneficiaries disagree, get legal advice before you list.

**Can I sell an inherited house to a family member?**
Yes, but at fair market value is safest, with an independent appraisal and the estate lawyer involved, to protect the executor and other beneficiaries.
