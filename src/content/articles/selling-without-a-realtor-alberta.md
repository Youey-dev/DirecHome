# How to sell your house privately in Alberta

**Short answer:** you can sell your home in Alberta without a realtor. You advertise and show it and talk price with buyers; an Alberta real estate lawyer handles the contract review, the deposit, the transfer and registration at Land Titles. Alberta has no land transfer tax, just a small Land Titles fee the buyer pays. The one thing to get started on early is your **Real Property Report** with your municipality's compliance stamp.

This guide covers what's specific to Alberta. For the steps that are the same across Canada (photos, safe viewings, advertising), see our [Canada-wide guide](/guides/selling-your-home).

---

## 1. Yes, you can sell your own home in Alberta

*Real estate licensing in Alberta covers people who sell for others, not owners.*

Real estate professionals in Alberta are licensed by the Real Estate Council of Alberta (RECA). As the owner, you don't need a licence to advertise, show or sell your own home.

You will need an Alberta real estate lawyer. Every Alberta sale, with or without an agent, closes through lawyers.

---

## 2. Hire your lawyer first

*Line them up before you list, so offers can go straight to them.*

Your lawyer usually:

- reviews or prepares the purchase contract and any counter-offers
- holds or confirms the buyer's deposit in trust
- gets your mortgage payout and pays off your lender
- prepares the statement of adjustments (property tax, condo fees, utilities)
- prepares the transfer of land and the dower documents, and registers the sale at Alberta Land Titles

Ask for a fixed-fee quote, and ask whether they've worked with a seller who has no agent. Then call your mortgage lender about any prepayment penalty: breaking a closed mortgage early can cost thousands.

> **Ask your lawyer.** How would you like to receive offers? Do I need a new Real Property Report, or can mine be updated? What should I tell buyers about the house?

---

## 3. Order your Real Property Report early

*It's the Alberta document most likely to hold up a private sale.*

A **Real Property Report (RPR)** is a legal plan, prepared and signed by an Alberta Land Surveyor, showing where your buildings and other visible improvements (garage, deck, fence, driveway) sit relative to your property lines.

Why it matters:

- Alberta buyers, their lawyers and lenders routinely expect the seller to provide a current RPR. Realtors' standard Alberta contracts require one, and buyers in a private sale will usually ask for it too.
- **Your municipality reviews it and stamps it for compliance** with local bylaws. That review can take a few weeks, so order it before you list.
- An RPR is a snapshot. If you've added or changed a deck, fence, garage or driveway since yours was done, it needs updating. Contact the surveyor who did the original: updating is often cheaper than starting over.
- If the RPR or the compliance review turns up a problem, such as a shed over the property line, sellers usually have to fix it or agree a solution before closing.

> **Ask your lawyer.** Is my existing RPR current enough, and what happens if the compliance stamp comes back with an issue?

---

## 4. Research your price

*Your municipal assessment is a starting point, not a price.*

Alberta municipalities reassess homes every year, and Calgary, Edmonton and many others let you look up assessments online. Use it as one reference alongside:

- recent sales of similar homes nearby (type, size, lot, age and condition)
- similar homes listed for sale now, which are your competition
- a paid appraisal, if you want an independent opinion

Pricing is your decision.

**Square footage in Alberta.** Licensed Alberta realtors must measure homes using RECA's Residential Measurement Standard (RMS). It doesn't bind private sellers, but buyers will compare your listing with RMS-measured ones. If you state a size, make sure it's accurate.

## 5. Alberta documents to gather

*The usual ones, plus a few Alberta specifics.*

| Document | Why Alberta buyers ask for it |
|---|---|
| **Real Property Report with compliance** | Shows the buildings are within the lot and meet local bylaws (see section 3) |
| Property tax notice and assessment | Part of the statement of adjustments, and buyers' budgets |
| Mortgage details | Your lawyer requests the payout statement |
| **Condo documents and estoppel certificate** (condos only) | Buyers review the bylaws, budget, reserve fund study and minutes. The **estoppel certificate** confirms your condo fees and anything owing. The corporation must provide it within 10 days of a written request, for up to $200 (a rush fee may apply if its bylaws allow). |
| Permits and receipts for major work | Buyers and inspectors ask; unpermitted work can show up in the compliance review |
| Well, septic and gas records (acreages) | Buyers' lenders often ask for water tests and septic information |

For the full stage-by-stage list, see our [paperwork checklist](/guides/paperwork-to-sell-your-house-privately).

**Disclosure.** Alberta doesn't require a standard seller disclosure form, but across Canada, hiding a known hidden problem that makes a home dangerous or unfit to live in can lead to a lawsuit after the sale. Write down every problem you know of and go through it with your lawyer.

## 6. If you're married: dower consent

*If your name is the only one on title, your spouse usually has to sign a dower document.*

Under Alberta's *Dower Act*, a married person's spouse has rights in the homestead, the home they live in, even if the spouse isn't on title. When only one owner sells, Alberta Land Titles needs one of these with the transfer:

- the spouse's **Consent and Acknowledgement** (signed in front of a lawyer or commissioner, apart from you)
- a **Dower Affidavit** confirming the home isn't subject to dower (for example, you're not married, or it isn't your homestead)
- a release of dower rights, or a court order

Your lawyer prepares the right one. Tell them early if you're married, separated, or the home is in one name only.

Separating or divorcing? See [Selling your house during a separation or divorce](/guides/selling-your-house-during-separation-or-divorce).

## 7. Offers in Alberta

*Every offer goes to your lawyer before you respond.*

The purchase contract sets out the price, deposit, included items, conditions and the closing (completion) day. Common Alberta conditions are financing, a home inspection, condo document review and, often, the RPR with compliance.

- You can accept, counter or decline.
- Once both sides sign and the buyer removes their conditions in writing, the sale is firm.
- Alberta doesn't have a BC-style 3-day buyer cancellation right for resale homes, so get legal advice before you sign.

**About "Alberta forms".** The standard Alberta contract you'll see mentioned is made by the realtors' association for its members. In a private sale, your lawyer decides what document to use.

> **Warning: deposits.** The buyer's deposit goes into a lawyer's or brokerage's trust account, as the contract says. Never accept a deposit yourself, by cash, cheque or e-Transfer.

> **Alberta note: home inspectors.** Home inspectors in Alberta must be licensed. Buyers usually arrange and pay for the inspection; let them in, and don't follow them around with commentary.

---

## 8. Land Titles fees (no land transfer tax)

*Alberta is one of the cheapest provinces to buy in, which helps your sale.*

Alberta has no land transfer tax. The buyer pays Land Titles registration fees instead:

- **Transfer of land:** $50, plus $5 for every $5,000 of the property's value
- **Mortgage:** $50, plus $5 for every $5,000 borrowed

On a $500,000 home, for example, the transfer fee is $50 + $500 = **$550**. These are the rates for registrations from October 1, 2026.

As the seller, you don't pay these fees. On the sale of your own principal residence there's generally no income tax on the gain, but you must report the sale on your tax return.

---

## 9. Closing in Alberta

*Your lawyer finishes the sale; you hand over the keys.*

- A few days before closing, you sign the transfer, dower document and closing papers at your lawyer's office (bring government photo ID).
- Your lawyer sends the signed transfer to the buyer's lawyer, who registers it at Land Titles. The sale money then flows to your lawyer, who pays off your mortgage and any other charges on title.
- Hand over keys, garage remotes and codes as the contract says, and leave the included items and a clean home.
- Your lawyer sends you the net proceeds and a final report.
- At tax time, report the sale on Schedule 3 and designate the home as your principal residence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can you sell a house without a realtor in Alberta?**
Yes. RECA licenses people who sell real estate for others; owners can sell their own homes. You'll still need an Alberta real estate lawyer to review the contract and close the sale.

**Do I need a lawyer to sell my house in Alberta?**
In practice, yes. Your lawyer handles the deposit, pays off your mortgage, prepares the transfer and dower documents, and works with the buyer's lawyer to register the sale at Land Titles.

**Do I need a Real Property Report to sell my house in Alberta?**
Buyers, their lawyers and lenders usually expect one, with your municipality's compliance stamp. It's best to order or update it before you list, because the compliance review can take a few weeks.

**How do I transfer ownership of a property title in Alberta?**
Your lawyer prepares a transfer of land, which you sign along with any dower document. The buyer's lawyer registers it at Alberta Land Titles once the sale money is in place. You don't file anything with Land Titles yourself.

**Is there a land transfer tax in Alberta?**
No. The buyer pays a Land Titles fee instead: $50 plus $5 for every $5,000 of the property's value for the transfer, and the same formula on the amount of their mortgage.

**What does it cost to sell a house privately in Alberta?**
Mainly your lawyer's fee, your RPR or its update and compliance, your mortgage discharge and any prepayment penalty, plus advertising (for example, $24.99 a month on OwnersMarket). If your buyer uses an agent, that agent may ask you to pay their commission, which would reduce your savings.
