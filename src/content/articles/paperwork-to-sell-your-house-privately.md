# Paperwork for selling your house privately in Canada: a stage-by-stage checklist

**Short answer:** selling privately doesn't mean doing the legal paperwork yourself. You gather the documents about your home (tax bill, mortgage details, renovation records, condo documents, a survey in some provinces). Your real estate lawyer prepares or reviews the legal ones: the agreement of purchase and sale, the transfer of title, the statement of adjustments and the closing documents.

Here's every document you're likely to meet, in the order you'll need it, with what it is and who handles it.

> **We don't provide contract forms or templates.** Your lawyer (or, in BC, your lawyer or notary) prepares or reviews every legal document in your sale. That's the safest way to sell, and it's what you'd pay for with or without an agent.

---

## 1. Before you list: documents about your home

*Gather these first. Buyers and their lawyers will ask for them.*

| Document | What it is | Where you get it |
|---|---|---|
| Property tax bill | Your latest annual bill and any arrears | Your municipality (or provincial tax office in some rural areas) |
| Property assessment | Your assessed value; useful for your own price research | Your provincial or city assessment office |
| Mortgage details | Your lender, account and term; your lawyer uses them to request a payout statement | Your mortgage statement or online banking |
| Utility and heating costs | A year of typical bills (power, gas or oil, water) | Your providers' online accounts |
| Renovation records | Permits, final inspections and receipts for major work (roof, furnace, windows, electrical) | Your own files; permit records from your municipality |
| Warranties and manuals | For appliances, roof, windows or a new-home warranty | Your own files |
| Rental contracts | For anything you rent and leave behind, like a water heater or furnace | The rental company |
| Survey or real property report | A drawing of the lot showing where the buildings sit | Your files or your lawyer; a land surveyor if you need a new one |
| Rural extras | Well water test, septic records, oil tank information | Your files, a water lab, a septic contractor |
| Condo documents | Bylaws, budget, financial statements, reserve fund study, recent minutes, and the corporation's certificate for buyers | Your condo's property manager (fees and timelines vary) |

**Disclosure.** In most provinces, sellers must tell buyers about known hidden defects that make a home dangerous or unfit to live in. Some provinces use a standard disclosure statement; others don't. Write down every problem you know of, fixed or not, and take the list to your lawyer.

> **Province note: Alberta.** Buyers usually expect a current Real Property Report with your municipality's compliance stamp. The stamp can take a few weeks, so order it before you list.

> **Province note: British Columbia.** For a strata (condo) home, the certificate for buyers is the Form B, and the reserve study is the depreciation report.

> **Province note: Ontario.** Condo buyers usually make their offer conditional on the condo's status certificate. Ask your property manager how long it takes.

> **Province note: Newfoundland and Labrador.** The province uses a registry of deeds, so your old deed, survey and past transfer documents are especially useful. Hire your lawyer early.

> **Ask your lawyer.** Bring your list of known defects to your first meeting and ask what you must disclose, and whether to use a disclosure statement.

## 2. While your home is listed

*Not much legal paperwork here, but keep a record.*

- **Your listing and feature sheet.** Keep a copy of what you advertised: price, room sizes, square footage, what's included. Buyers rely on it, so it needs to be accurate.
- **Viewing records.** Who viewed, when, and their contact details. On OwnersMarket, viewing requests and messages are kept on record for you.
- **Any agreement with a buyer's agent.** If you agree to pay a buyer's agent commission, put it in writing with the agent's brokerage before the viewing, and have your lawyer look at it first.

---

## 3. The offer

*This is where your lawyer takes over the paperwork.*

| Document | What it is | Who prepares it |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement of purchase and sale (called a contract of purchase and sale in some provinces) | The legally binding contract: price, deposit, conditions, what's included, closing and possession dates | Usually a lawyer: the buyer's lawyer drafts it and yours reviews it, or yours prepares it |
| Schedules | Extra terms attached to the agreement, such as conditions or a list of included items | The lawyer preparing the agreement |
| Counter-offers and amendments | Your written changes to the buyer's offer, and theirs back | Your lawyer drafts or reviews them |
| Deposit receipt | Proof the buyer's deposit was paid into a lawyer's trust account | The lawyer holding the deposit |

**About standard forms.** You'll see "standard offer forms" mentioned online. Most are forms made by real estate associations for their members. In a private sale, your lawyer decides what document to use.

> **Province note: Manitoba.** Manitoba's real estate regulation sets out a standard Residential Offer to Purchase Contract that brokerages use. It's public on the government's website. Ask your lawyer whether to use it for your private sale; they'll fill it in or review it with you.

> **Province note: British Columbia.** After you accept an offer, the buyer has 3 business days to cancel (the home buyer rescission period). If they cancel, they pay you 0.25% of the price. This applies to private sales too.

> **Warning: never take a deposit yourself.** The deposit goes into a lawyer's (or BC notary's) trust account, as the agreement says. Don't accept cash, e-Transfers or cheques made out to you.

---

## 4. Conditions: what the buyer may ask you for

*The buyer usually has a few days or weeks to satisfy their conditions.*

- **Access for a home inspection**, and sometimes for the lender's appraiser.
- **Condo documents** and the corporation's certificate, if they haven't seen them.
- **Your survey or Real Property Report.**
- **Answers to their lawyer's questions** about the title, any work done, or included items.

When the buyer is satisfied, they sign a written notice removing (or waiving) their conditions. From then on, the sale is firm. If a condition isn't met by its deadline, the agreement usually ends; your lawyer will explain what your agreement says.

---

## 5. Closing: documents your lawyer prepares

*You'll sign some of these at your lawyer's office a few days before closing.*

| Document | What it is |
|---|---|
| Transfer (deed) | The document that moves ownership to the buyer, registered at your province's land titles office or registry |
| Statement of adjustments | Who owes what for property taxes, condo fees and other prepaid costs, as of closing day |
| Mortgage payout statement and discharge | Your lender's figure to pay off your mortgage, and the document removing it from your title afterwards |
| Declarations | Sworn statements, for example that you're not a non-resident of Canada, or about your marital status and the home |
| Undertakings | Promises your lawyer gives the buyer's lawyer, such as to pay off and discharge your mortgage |
| Keys and possession | Arrangements for handing over keys, garage remotes and codes on possession day |

**If you don't live in Canada.** A non-resident seller must notify the Canada Revenue Agency within 10 days of the sale and get a certificate of compliance. Without it, the buyer can hold back 25% of the price (more for some property types). Tell your lawyer right away if this applies to you.

> **Ask your lawyer.** When do you need me to sign? What ID should I bring? When will the sale money reach my account?

---

## 6. After the sale

*Keep copies, and don't forget your tax return.*

- **Your tax return.** If the home was your principal residence, report the sale on Schedule 3 and designate it on Form T2091(IND). If it was your principal residence every year you owned it, the gain is generally not taxed.
- **Short ownership.** If you owned the home for less than 365 days, the federal flipping rule may treat your profit as business income unless an exception applies. In BC, the home flipping tax may also apply to homes owned for under 730 days.
- **Your closing package.** Keep the agreement, statement of adjustments and your lawyer's report in a safe place.
- **Cancel and redirect.** Home insurance and utilities from the possession date; mail forwarding.

## Frequently asked questions

**What documents do I need to sell my house privately?**
From you: your property tax bill, mortgage details, utility costs, records of renovations, any survey, and condo documents if it's a condo. From your lawyer: the agreement of purchase and sale (or their review of the buyer's), the transfer, the statement of adjustments and the closing documents.

**Is there a standard contract for selling a house privately?**
Not one you should fill in alone. The forms you see online are mostly made by real estate associations for their members. In a private sale, the buyer's lawyer or yours prepares the agreement, and both lawyers review it. In Manitoba, a standard brokerage form is set out in regulation; ask your lawyer whether to use it.

**Do I need a lawyer to sell my house privately?**
In practice, yes. A lawyer (or a notary in BC) transfers the title, pays off your mortgage and makes sure the money changes hands safely. You'd need one even with an agent.

**Who prepares the deed when you sell a house?**
Your lawyer prepares the transfer (often called the deed) and registers it at the provincial land titles office or registry on closing day.

**What do I need to bring to my lawyer to close?**
Usually government photo ID, your mortgage details, the keys and any remotes, and the documents they've asked for, such as your tax bill or condo certificate. Your lawyer will send you a list.

**Do I have to tell the CRA that I sold my house?**
Yes. Report the sale on Schedule 3 of your tax return and designate it as your principal residence. If you're not a resident of Canada, there's an extra CRA notice and certificate to get before closing.
