# How to sell your house privately in Ontario

**Short answer:** you can sell your own home in Ontario without a realtor. You advertise it, show it and talk price with buyers; a real estate lawyer handles the agreement, the deposit, the title transfer and closing, which in Ontario is registered electronically. The buyer pays Ontario's land transfer tax (and, in Toronto, the city's too), so as the seller your main costs are your lawyer, your mortgage discharge and advertising.

This guide covers what's specific to Ontario. For the steps that are the same everywhere in Canada (photos, safe viewings, advertising), see our [Canada-wide guide](/guides/selling-your-home).

---

## 1. Yes, private sales are allowed in Ontario

*Ontario's real estate licensing rules apply to people who sell for others, not to owners selling their own home.*

Real estate agents and brokerages in Ontario are regulated by RECO under the *Trust in Real Estate Services Act, 2002*. Those rules govern professionals. As the owner, you don't need a licence to advertise, show or sell your own home.

What you do need is an Ontario real estate lawyer. That's true in every Ontario sale, with or without an agent.

---

## 2. Your lawyer closes the sale

*In Ontario, real estate closings are done by lawyers, and registration is electronic.*

Your lawyer usually:

- reviews or prepares the agreement of purchase and sale, and any counter-offers
- holds or confirms the buyer's deposit in a trust account
- gets your mortgage payout statement and pays off your lender on closing
- prepares the statement of adjustments (property tax, condo fees, prepaid utilities)
- signs and registers the transfer electronically in Ontario's land registry on closing day

The buyer hires their own lawyer, who searches the title and arranges their mortgage and title insurance.

**Hire yours before you list.** Ask for a fixed-fee quote for a sale, and ask whether they're comfortable working with a seller who has no agent. Then call your mortgage lender about any prepayment penalty: breaking a closed mortgage early can cost thousands.

> **Ask your lawyer.** How would you like me to send you offers? Will you draft the agreement, or review the buyer's lawyer's draft? What do I need to tell buyers about the house?

---

## 3. Research your price (with one Ontario caution)

*Your MPAC assessment is a starting point, not a price.*

Every Ontario property is assessed by the Municipal Property Assessment Corporation (MPAC). Check the **valuation date** on your notice: because Ontario postponed its province-wide reassessment, current assessments have been based on **January 1, 2016** values. For most homes, today's market is very different.

Better guides to price:

- recent sales of similar homes nearby (type, size, lot, age and condition)
- similar homes listed for sale now, which are your competition
- a paid appraisal from a certified appraiser, if you want an independent opinion

Pricing is your decision. Homes priced close to recent sales usually get the most viewing requests early on.

## 4. Ontario documents to gather

*Most of these are the same as anywhere. A few are Ontario-specific.*

| Document | Why Ontario buyers ask for it |
|---|---|
| Property tax bill and MPAC notice | Part of the statement of adjustments, and buyers' budgets |
| Mortgage details | Your lawyer requests the payout statement |
| **Condo status certificate** (condos only) | Buyers almost always make the offer conditional on reviewing it. The condo must provide it within 10 days of the request and payment, for no more than $100 including tax (faster service can cost more). |
| **Rental equipment contracts** | Water heaters, furnaces and air conditioners are often rented in Ontario. The agreement must say who takes over the rental, and rental companies sometimes register a notice on the title. Your lawyer deals with it on closing. |
| **Tarion warranty details** (newer homes) | Nearly every new Ontario home has a seven-year warranty backed by Tarion. If yours is still covered, the buyer can contact Tarion to be added as the new owner, using the agreement and the transfer. |
| Survey, if you have one | Not always required; many Ontario buyers rely on title insurance instead. Have it ready if you have one. |
| Well and septic records (rural homes) | Lenders and buyers commonly ask for water tests and septic information. |
| Permits and receipts for major work | Buyers and inspectors ask; unpermitted work raises questions. |

For the full stage-by-stage list, see our [paperwork checklist](/guides/paperwork-to-sell-your-house-privately).

## 5. What you have to disclose in Ontario

*Ontario has no mandatory disclosure form, but you can't hide known problems.*

Ontario doesn't require sellers to fill in a standard disclosure statement. That doesn't mean "say nothing". Across Canada, a seller who knows about a hidden problem that makes the home dangerous or unfit to live in, and conceals it, can be sued after the sale. Lying in answer to a buyer's question, or in your listing, is never safe.

Practical approach: write down every problem you know of (water in the basement, past insect or pest treatment, old wiring, a buried oil tank), fixed or not, and go through the list with your lawyer before you list.

> **Ask your lawyer.** Should I give buyers a written disclosure, and what should it say about the problems on my list?

## 6. If you're married: your spouse's consent

*Even if your spouse isn't on the title, they usually have to sign.*

Under Ontario's *Family Law Act*, both spouses have rights in the matrimonial home, the home they ordinarily live in as a family. When a matrimonial home is sold, the transfer includes a statement about the seller's spouse, and in most cases the spouse signs their consent, even if only one of you owns it.

Tell your lawyer early if you're married, separated, or the home is in one name only, so it doesn't hold up closing.

---

## 7. Offers in Ontario

*Every offer goes to your lawyer before you respond.*

In Ontario the contract is called an **agreement of purchase and sale**. It sets out the price, deposit, included items, conditions and the closing date. Common conditions in Ontario:

- **financing**: the buyer's mortgage approval
- **home inspection**
- **status certificate review** (condos)
- **lawyer's review**

You can accept, counter or decline. Once you've both signed and the buyer has removed (or waived) every condition in writing, the sale is firm.

**About "Ontario forms".** You'll find standard Ontario forms online, mostly made by the real estate association for its members. In a private sale, your lawyer decides what document to use; don't fill one in on your own.

> **Province note: no BC-style cancellation right.** Unlike British Columbia, Ontario doesn't give buyers of resale homes a 3-day right to cancel after signing. That makes it even more important that your lawyer reviews the offer before you sign.

> **Warning: deposits.** The buyer's deposit goes into a lawyer's or brokerage's trust account, as the agreement says. Never accept a deposit yourself, by cash, cheque or e-Transfer.

---

## 8. Who pays land transfer tax in Ontario

*The buyer pays. It still affects your sale, because it's part of their budget.*

The buyer pays Ontario's land transfer tax when the sale is registered. For a home with one or two single-family residences:

- 0.5% on the first $55,000
- 1% from $55,000 to $250,000
- 1.5% from $250,000 to $400,000
- 2% from $400,000 to $2,000,000
- 2.5% on the part above $2,000,000

First-time buyers can get a refund of up to $4,000. In **Toronto**, buyers also pay the City's municipal land transfer tax on top. Buyers who aren't Canadian citizens or permanent residents generally also pay Ontario's 25% Non-Resident Speculation Tax, which applies province-wide; their lawyer will know if an exemption applies.

As the seller, you don't pay land transfer tax. On the sale of your own principal residence there's generally no income tax either, but you must report the sale on your tax return.

---

## 9. Closing day and after

*Your lawyer finishes the sale online; you hand over the keys.*

- A few days before closing, you sign the transfer and closing documents at your lawyer's office (bring government photo ID).
- On closing day, the lawyers exchange documents and money, your lawyer pays off your mortgage, and the transfer is registered electronically.
- You hand over keys, garage remotes and codes as the agreement says. Leave the included items and the home clean.
- Your lawyer sends you the net proceeds and a final report.
- At tax time, report the sale on Schedule 3 and designate the home as your principal residence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I sell my house without a realtor in Ontario?**
Yes. Ontario's licensing rules cover people who sell homes for others, not owners selling their own. You'll still need an Ontario real estate lawyer to handle the agreement and closing, as in any sale.

**Do I need a lawyer to sell my house in Ontario?**
In practice, yes. Ontario closings, including the electronic registration of the transfer, are handled by lawyers. Hire yours before you list, so offers can go straight to them.

**How much tax do you pay when you sell your house in Ontario?**
If it was your principal residence for every year you owned it, generally none on the gain, and the buyer pays the land transfer tax. You still need to report the sale on your tax return. Different rules can apply if you rented it out or owned it for under a year; ask an accountant.

**Who pays land transfer tax in Ontario, the buyer or the seller?**
The buyer. In Toronto, the buyer pays both the provincial and the municipal land transfer tax. First-time buyers can get a provincial refund of up to $4,000.

**Is there a cooling-off period when you sell a resale home in Ontario?**
Not like British Columbia's 3-day rescission period. Once both sides sign and the buyer's conditions are removed, the sale is firm. That's why every offer should go to your lawyer before you sign.

**What does selling a house privately in Ontario cost?**
Mainly your lawyer's fee, your mortgage discharge fee and any prepayment penalty, plus advertising (for example, $24.99 a month on OwnersMarket). If your buyer uses an agent, that agent may ask you to pay their commission, which would reduce your savings.
