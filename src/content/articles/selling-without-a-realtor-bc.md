# How to sell your own home in BC without a realtor

**Short answer:** you can sell your own home in British Columbia without a realtor. You advertise it, show it and talk price with buyers; a lawyer **or a notary public** handles the contract, the deposit, the transfer at the Land Title Office and completion. Two BC rules to know before you list: you must disclose known **material latent defects** in writing, and after you accept an offer the buyer has **3 business days to cancel** (the rescission period), even in a private sale. The buyer, not you, pays BC's property transfer tax.

This guide covers what's specific to BC. For the steps that are the same across Canada (photos, safe viewings, advertising), see our [Canada-wide guide](/guides/selling-your-home).

---

## 1. Yes, you can sell your own home in BC

*Real estate licensing in BC covers people who sell for others, not owners.*

Real estate professionals in BC are licensed and regulated by the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA). As the owner, you don't need a licence to advertise, show or sell your own home.

What you do need is a lawyer or a notary for the legal side, as in any BC sale.

---

## 2. Hire a lawyer or a notary first

*BC is one of the few provinces where a notary can handle your sale.*

In BC, either a **lawyer** or a **notary public** can act for you on a residential sale. They usually:

- prepare or review the contract of purchase and sale, and any counter-offers
- hold the buyer's deposit in their trust account
- get your mortgage payout and pay off your lender
- prepare the statement of adjustments (property tax, strata fees, utilities)
- prepare the transfer documents and register the new owner at the Land Title Office on completion day

The buyer hires their own. Their lawyer or notary also files the buyer's property transfer tax return.

Ask for a fixed-fee quote, and ask whether they've acted for a seller with no agent. Then call your mortgage lender about any prepayment penalty: breaking a closed mortgage early can cost thousands.

> **Ask your lawyer or notary.** How would you like to receive offers? Will you prepare the contract, or review the buyer's? What do I need to disclose about my home? How should my contact details appear in the contract for the rescission period?

---

## 3. Research your price (BC Assessment is a starting point)

*Your assessment reflects last July, not today.*

BC Assessment values every property in the province, and you can look yours up for free. BC Assessment's values reflect the market **as of July 1 of the previous year**, so the market may have moved since. Use it as one reference alongside:

- recent sales of similar homes nearby (type, size, lot, age and condition)
- similar homes listed for sale now, which are your competition
- a paid appraisal, if you want an independent opinion

Pricing is your decision. Our guide to [researching your asking price](/guides/how-to-research-your-asking-price) walks through the method.

---

## 4. What you must disclose in BC

*Known hidden defects, in writing, before a contract is signed.*

BC's regulator, BCFSA, describes a **material latent defect** as a problem that can't be discovered on a reasonable inspection and that may make the home dangerous or unfit to live in, or would be expensive to fix. Sellers must tell buyers in writing about any material latent defects they know of **before** a contract is signed, in a document separate from the contract.

Examples to think about: water getting into the basement, structural damage, an old buried oil tank, or electrical or gas work done without permits.

Many BC sellers also give buyers a **Property Disclosure Statement**, a standard set of questions about the home. Whatever you use, answer honestly: hiding a known problem can lead to a lawsuit after the sale.

> **Ask your lawyer or notary.** How should I disclose the problems on my list, and should I use a Property Disclosure Statement?

---

## 5. Selling a strata home? Order the Form B early

*Buyers almost always make their offer subject to the strata documents.*

For a strata (condo or townhouse) home, buyers will want:

- the **Information Certificate (Form B)** from your strata corporation. The strata must provide it within **7 days** of a request, for no more than **$35** plus up to 25 cents a page for copies (a rush fee may apply if you ask with less notice). It covers your strata fees and anything you owe, approved special levies, the contingency reserve fund, legal proceedings, and parking and storage allocations, with the rules, budget and most recent depreciation report attached.
- the **bylaws and rules**, recent **council and annual general meeting minutes**, and the **financial statements**
- the **depreciation report**, which looks at upcoming repairs and the reserve fund

Before completion, your lawyer or notary will also need a **Certificate of Payment (Form F)** showing you owe nothing to the strata. The Land Title Office requires it to transfer a strata lot. It's valid for 60 days, must be provided within 7 days, and costs no more than $15.

For the full stage-by-stage list, see our [paperwork checklist](/guides/paperwork-to-sell-your-house-privately).

## 6. Offers: the contract of purchase and sale

*In BC, conditions are "subjects" and closing is "completion".*

A BC offer is a written **contract of purchase and sale**. It sets out the price, the deposit, the buyer's **subjects** (such as financing, an inspection and strata documents) and three dates:

- **completion:** when the money and title change hands
- **possession:** when the buyer gets the keys
- **adjustment:** from when the buyer takes over property taxes and strata fees

What to do:

- Send every offer to your lawyer or notary before you respond.
- You can accept, counter or decline.
- Once the buyer removes their subjects in writing, the sale is firm, subject to the rescission period below.

**About "BC forms".** The standard contract you'll see online is the real estate industry's form, used by its members. In a private sale, your lawyer or notary decides what document to use.

> **Warning: deposits.** The deposit goes into the trust account named in the contract (a lawyer's, a notary's or, if the buyer has an agent, a brokerage's). Never accept a deposit yourself, by cash, cheque or e-Transfer.

## 7. The buyer's 3-day right to cancel

*BC's home buyer rescission period applies to private sales too.*

After you accept an offer on most BC homes, the buyer has **3 business days** (not counting weekends and statutory holidays) to cancel without giving a reason. If they cancel, they pay you a fee of **0.25% of the price**: $2,000 on an $800,000 home. Neither side can waive it or change the amount, and it applies whether or not an agent is involved.

Good to know:

- **It doesn't apply to every property:** for example, leasehold properties and homes on leased land (including manufactured homes on leased pads), and sales at auction or under a court order are exempt.
- **In a private sale**, the buyer gives you written notice, so make sure your email and mailing address in the contract are correct.
- **The fee:** if a brokerage holds the deposit, it pays the fee to you from the deposit. If there's no brokerage involved, ask your lawyer or notary how the fee will be handled, and consider that when setting where the deposit is held.

> **Ask your lawyer or notary.** How will a rescission notice reach me, and how will I receive the 0.25% fee if the buyer cancels?

## 8. If you're married or separating

*BC has no automatic consent rule, with one exception.*

Unlike Ontario or Alberta, BC doesn't automatically require your spouse's signature to sell a home in your name alone. The exception: under BC's *Land (Spouse Protection) Act*, a spouse can register an entry on the title saying the home is protected. Once that entry is registered, you can't sell without your spouse's consent.

If you're separating, or your spouse has an interest in the home, talk to a family lawyer before you list: family property rules can affect what you're able to sell and how the money is divided.

Separating or divorcing? See [Selling your house during a separation or divorce](/guides/selling-your-house-during-separation-or-divorce).

## 9. Property transfer tax (paid by the buyer)

*It's not your cost, but it's part of your buyer's budget.*

The buyer pays BC's property transfer tax when the home is registered in their name, based on fair market value:

- 1% on the first $200,000
- 2% from $200,000 to $2,000,000
- 3% above $2,000,000
- a further 2% on the residential value above $3,000,000

**First-time buyers** can be exempt: no tax on homes worth up to $500,000, a reduced amount up to $835,000, phasing out completely at $860,000 (for registrations since April 1, 2024). Foreign buyers may owe an additional tax in some areas of BC; their lawyer will advise them.

---

## 10. Taxes when you sell in BC

*Most owners selling their own home pay no tax on the sale, but check two rules.*

- **BC home flipping tax:** in effect since January 1, 2025. If you sell a home you've owned for less than **730 days**, part of your profit may be taxed (20% if you owned it for under a year, decreasing after that). If it was your principal residence for at least 365 days, you can deduct up to $20,000. You must file a BC home flipping tax return **within 90 days** of the sale, generally even if no tax is owed.
- **Federal flipping rule:** if you owned the home for less than 365 days, the profit may be taxed as business income unless a life-event exception applies.
- **Report the sale** on your federal tax return (Schedule 3) and designate the home as your principal residence, even when no tax is owed.

> **Ask an accountant** before you list if you've owned the home for less than 2 years, rented part of it out, or own it with someone else.

---

## 11. Completion and possession

*Your lawyer or notary finishes the sale; you hand over the keys.*

- A few days before completion, you sign the transfer and closing documents at your lawyer's or notary's office (bring government photo ID).
- On completion day, the buyer's funds arrive, the transfer is registered at the Land Title Office, and your lawyer or notary pays off your mortgage.
- On possession day, hand over every key, fob and remote, and leave the included items and a clean home.
- Your lawyer or notary sends you the net proceeds and a statement showing every amount.

**Home inspectors in BC must be licensed** by Consumer Protection BC. Buyers usually book and pay for the inspection; let them in, and you can check an inspector's licence on the regulator's website.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I sell my house without a realtor in BC?**
Yes. BC's licensing rules cover people who sell homes for others, not owners selling their own. You'll still need a lawyer or notary to handle the contract, the deposit and the transfer at the Land Title Office.

**Does the BC 3-day rescission period apply to private sales?**
Yes, for most residential properties. After you accept an offer, the buyer has 3 business days to cancel and pays you 0.25% of the price if they do. Leasehold properties, homes on leased land and some other sales are exempt.

**Who pays property transfer tax in BC, the buyer or the seller?**
The buyer. It's 1% on the first $200,000 and 2% up to $2 million, with higher rates above that. First-time buyers can be exempt on homes up to $500,000 and partly exempt up to $860,000.

**What forms do I need to sell my house privately in BC?**
Mostly documents rather than forms: your property tax notice, mortgage details, a written disclosure of known defects and, for a strata home, the Form B and Form F. Your lawyer or notary prepares or reviews the contract of purchase and sale and the transfer documents.

**Can a notary handle my house sale in BC?**
Yes. In BC, a notary public can handle a residential sale instead of a lawyer: reviewing the contract, holding the deposit, paying out your mortgage and registering the transfer.

**Do I have to pay tax when I sell my house in BC?**
Usually not, if it was your principal residence the whole time you owned it, but you must report the sale. If you owned it for less than 2 years, the BC home flipping tax may apply, and you may need to file a return within 90 days even if you owe nothing.
