# Should I sell my house myself? An honest look at the pros and cons

**Short answer:** you can legally sell your own home anywhere in Canada without a realtor, and you won't pay a listing commission. It works best when your home is move-in ready, you can open the door for viewings, and you hire a real estate lawyer early. If you want someone else to set your price, negotiate and manage the sale for you, an agent is the better fit.

Below is an honest look at both sides, a quick checklist, and the middle-ground options, so you can decide what suits you and your home.

---

## 1. Yes, you're allowed to sell your own home

*No licence is needed to sell a home you own. The legal side still needs a professional.*

Real estate licences in Canada are for people who sell homes **for other people**. Selling your own home privately is legal in every province and territory.

What doesn't change is the legal work. Whoever sells, a real estate lawyer (or, in British Columbia, a lawyer or notary public) checks the title, prepares the transfer documents, holds and pays out the money, pays off your mortgage and registers the new owner. You'd pay for this with or without an agent, so it isn't an extra cost of selling privately.

So the real question isn't "am I allowed?" It's "do I want to do the jobs an agent would normally do?"

---

## 2. What you could save (an estimate)

*The listing commission is the big saving. Whether you also pay a buyer's agent depends on your buyer.*

Real estate commissions in Canada aren't set by law. The federal Financial Consumer Agency of Canada notes they're negotiable and usually range from about 2% to 6% depending on where you live.

Here's an **example only**, using a common rate structure: 7% on the first $100,000, 2.5% on the rest, plus 5% GST.

| On a $650,000 home | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Total commission at the example rate | about **$21,788** |
| Usually split: listing side | about $10,894 |
| Usually split: buyer's agent side | about $10,894 |

If you sell privately and your buyer **doesn't** use an agent, you could keep roughly the whole amount, before your own costs. If your buyer **does** use an agent, that agent may ask you to pay their commission, which would reduce your savings (in this example, to roughly half).

Your own costs of selling privately are usually small by comparison: your lawyer (which you'd pay anyway), photos if you hire a photographer, a sign, and somewhere to advertise. On OwnersMarket, for example, advertising costs $24.99 a month, with no commission.

> **Estimate only.** Commissions are negotiable and vary by province. This isn't financial advice; your real savings depend on your price, your buyer and your costs.

## 3. The advantages of selling yourself

*You keep more of your equity and stay in control of the sale.*

- **No listing commission.** On most Canadian homes this is thousands to tens of thousands of dollars.
- **You know your home best.** You can answer buyers' questions about the furnace, the neighbours and the school bus first-hand.
- **You choose the viewings.** You decide when people come through and who you meet.
- **Direct conversations.** Buyers talk to you, not through a chain of messages, so questions get answered quickly.
- **You set the pace.** No listing agreement pushing you to drop the price on someone else's schedule.

---

## 4. The downsides and risks

*It's more work, and a few things are genuinely harder without an agent.*

- **Time.** Photos, the listing, answering messages and showing the home are now your jobs, often on evenings and weekends.
- **Pricing is on you.** You'll need to research recent sales yourself. Overpricing is the most common reason a private listing sits.
- **Negotiating your own home is emotional.** Hearing a low offer on the house you raised your kids in is hard. Your lawyer handles the legal side of an offer, but they won't haggle on price for you.
- **Fewer eyes, at first.** Private listings generally can't go on the MLS (the realtor database) unless you pay a brokerage to list it. Some buyers only look there.
- **Buyers' agents.** Some buyers work with an agent who will want to be paid. Decide in advance whether you're open to that, and talk to your lawyer before agreeing to anything in writing.
- **Safety.** You'll be letting strangers into your home.

> **Safety first.** Confirm a viewer's full name and phone number before you share your address. Have someone else home during viewings, put away valuables and prescriptions, and never accept a deposit yourself: deposits go to a lawyer's (or BC notary's) trust account.

## 5. Is selling yourself a good fit? A quick checklist

*Three or four "yes" answers on the left is a good sign.*

| Good fit if… | Probably not a good fit if… |
|---|---|
| Your home is move-in ready, with no big repairs to explain | The home needs major work, or has problems that are hard to explain |
| You can open the door on evenings or weekends | You've already moved away, or can't be there for viewings |
| You have, or will hire, a real estate lawyer (or notary in BC) | You'd rather not deal with any of the legal side, even through a lawyer |
| You're comfortable answering questions and talking about price | Talking money with strangers makes you very uncomfortable |
| Similar homes nearby sell regularly, so price research is possible | Your home is unusual (acreage, heritage, very high-end) with few comparable sales |
| You have some flexibility on timing | You must sell by a fixed date, such as before a new home's closing |

There's no wrong answer here. Plenty of people try privately first; others go straight to an agent and are glad they did.

## 6. What jobs you take on (and who does them)

*Selling privately isn't doing everything yourself: it's splitting the work between you and your lawyer.*

| Job | With a listing agent | Selling privately |
|---|---|---|
| Researching an asking price | Agent suggests a price | You research recent sales and assessments |
| Photos and listing description | Agent arranges | You, or a photographer you hire |
| Advertising the home | Agent (MLS and their marketing) | You: a listing site, a sign, social media |
| Answering buyers and booking viewings | Agent | You |
| Negotiating the price | Agent | You, directly with the buyer or their agent |
| Contract, conditions, deposit, title transfer, closing | Agent and lawyer | **Your lawyer** (or notary in BC) |

> **Ask your lawyer.** Call a real estate lawyer before you list. Ask what they charge for a sale, how they'd like to receive offers, and what you need to disclose about your home. Having them lined up means you can act quickly when an offer arrives.

> **In British Columbia:** after you accept an offer, the buyer has 3 business days to cancel, even in a private sale. If they cancel, they pay you 0.25% of the price. See our [BC guide](/guides/selling-without-a-realtor-bc) for the other BC rules.

## 7. The middle-ground options

*It isn't all-or-nothing. Here's the honest range.*

- **Sell privately:** advertise on a for-sale-by-owner site (such as OwnersMarket, PropertyGuys or ComFree), Kijiji, Facebook Marketplace and a lawn sign. Lowest cost; you do the most.
- **Flat-fee MLS listing:** you pay a brokerage a set fee to put your home on the MLS, and still handle viewings yourself. More exposure, and buyers' agents will usually expect to be paid.
- **Discount or reduced-commission brokerage:** an agent does part or all of the job for a lower commission.
- **Full-service agent:** the agent prices, markets and negotiates for you. Remember that commissions are negotiable.

For a side-by-side look, see [Selling privately vs a discount brokerage vs a flat-fee MLS listing](/guides/selling-privately-vs-discount-brokerage-vs-flat-fee-mls).

Many sellers **try privately for a few weeks**, then list with an agent if it isn't working. If you do, read the listing agreement carefully: some include a clause that you owe commission if a buyer the agent introduced buys after the agreement ends. Ask before you sign.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it worth selling your house privately?**
It can be, especially for a move-in-ready home in an area where similar homes sell regularly. You avoid the listing commission, which is often thousands to tens of thousands of dollars. It's less worth it if you can't be available for viewings, your home is hard to price, or you need to sell by a fixed date.

**Is it hard to sell a house without a realtor?**
The work isn't complicated, but there's more of it: photos, the listing, messages, viewings and negotiating. The legal parts (the contract, deposit, title transfer and closing) are handled by your lawyer, just as they would be with an agent. Most people find pricing and negotiating the hardest parts.

**Can I sell my house without a lawyer?**
In practice, no. In Canada a lawyer (or, in BC, a lawyer or notary) handles the title transfer, pays off your mortgage and makes sure the money changes hands safely. That's true whether or not you use an agent.

**What if the buyer has a real estate agent?**
That's common. The buyer's agent may ask you to pay their commission. You can agree, negotiate or decline, but put any agreement in writing and run it past your lawyer first. Some sellers say up front that they're open to buyers' agents to widen their pool of buyers.

**Can I list my house on the MLS without a realtor?**
Not directly. The MLS is run by real estate boards for their members. You can pay a flat-fee brokerage to list it there for you, or advertise on for-sale-by-owner sites, classifieds and social media instead.

**Do I have to tell the CRA that I sold my house?**
Yes. If the home was your principal residence, you report the sale on Schedule 3 of your tax return and designate it as your principal residence. If it was your principal residence for every year you owned it, the gain is generally not taxed. Ask an accountant if you rented it out or owned it with others.
