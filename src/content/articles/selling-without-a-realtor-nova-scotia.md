# How to sell your house privately in Nova Scotia

**Short answer:** you can sell your own home in Nova Scotia without a realtor. You advertise it, show it and talk price with buyers; a Nova Scotia lawyer handles the agreement, the deposit, the title and closing. If your property hasn't been sold since Nova Scotia's land registration system started, it has to be converted on this sale, so allow a week or two extra before closing. The buyer pays a municipal deed transfer tax of 1% to 1.5%.

This guide covers what's specific to Nova Scotia. For the steps that are the same across Canada (photos, safe viewings, advertising), see our [Canada-wide guide](/guides/selling-your-home).

---

## 1. Yes, you can sell your own home in Nova Scotia

*Real estate licensing covers people who act for others.*

The Nova Scotia Real Estate Commission licenses people who trade in real estate, which it describes as acting for a consumer to buy or sell property. As the owner, you can advertise, show and sell your own home.

You'll need a Nova Scotia lawyer for the legal side, as in any sale.

---

## 2. Hire your lawyer first

*Line them up before you list, especially if your property needs converting.*

Your lawyer usually:

- prepares or reviews the agreement of purchase and sale and any counter-offers
- holds the buyer's deposit in trust
- certifies your title and handles any conversion to the land registration system (see section 3)
- gets your mortgage payout and pays off your lender
- prepares the statement of adjustments (property tax, condo fees, oil or propane in the tank)
- registers the deed with the buyer's lawyer on closing day

Ask for a fixed-fee quote, including any conversion work, and ask whether they've acted for a seller with no agent. Then call your mortgage lender about any prepayment penalty: breaking a closed mortgage early can cost thousands.

> **Ask your lawyer.** Has my property been converted to the land registration system? If not, what will conversion cost and how long will it add? How would you like to receive offers?

---

## 3. Land registration: does your property need converting?

*A one-time job that can add a week or two to the sale.*

Nova Scotia is moving properties from the old deed registry to its land registration system. A property **must be converted when it's sold**. During conversion, a lawyer reviews the title history, certifies the title to the government and registers the parcel; a surveyor or lawyer certifies the description.

What it means for you:

- **Timing:** the province suggests sellers allow one or two extra weeks in the agreement of purchase and sale.
- **Cost:** the province adds no conversion fee beyond the usual filing fee, but your lawyer (and a surveyor, if needed) will charge for the work.
- **Who pays:** either the seller or the buyer can pay for conversion; it's settled in the agreement. Ask your lawyer what's usual where you live.
- **Already converted?** Your lawyer can tell you in minutes. If so, there's no conversion step.

---

## 4. Research your price

*Your assessment is a reference, not a price.*

Property Valuation Services Corporation (PVSC) assesses every property in Nova Scotia each year. Use your assessed value as one reference alongside:

- recent sales of similar homes nearby (type, size, lot, age and condition)
- similar homes listed for sale now, which are your competition
- a paid appraisal, if you want an independent opinion

Pricing is your decision.

> **Nova Scotia note: the assessment cap.** If you've owned your home for a while, your **taxable** assessment may be capped under PVSC's Capped Assessment Program, so your tax bill is lower than the market value suggests. The cap is removed for the year after a sale (unless the buyer is a family member), so your buyer's property tax may be noticeably higher than yours. Share your tax bill, but expect buyers to ask about it.

## 5. Nova Scotia documents to gather

*Mostly the usual list, plus a few Nova Scotia habits.*

| Document | Why Nova Scotia buyers ask for it |
|---|---|
| Property tax bill and PVSC assessment | Part of the statement of adjustments; see the cap note above |
| Mortgage details | Your lawyer requests the payout statement |
| Deed, survey and any old title documents | Helpful if your property needs converting |
| **Heating oil or propane details** | Many Nova Scotia homes heat with oil; buyers and insurers ask about the tank's age and location |
| Well and septic records (rural homes) | Lenders and buyers commonly ask for a water test and septic information |
| Condo documents and certificate (condos only) | Buyers review the declaration, bylaws, budget and reserve fund; ask your property manager about the certificate for buyers, its fee and timing |
| Permits and receipts for major work | Buyers and inspectors ask |

## 6. Disclosure in Nova Scotia

*Not required by form, but expected by buyers.*

In Nova Scotia, licensed brokerages use a standard **Property Disclosure Statement** when listing. The Nova Scotia Real Estate Commission notes that it's up to sellers whether they give buyers a disclosure statement, and most do. It creates a written record of what you said about the home, which can prevent misunderstandings later.

Whatever you decide, don't hide known problems. Across Canada, concealing a hidden defect that makes a home dangerous or unfit to live in can lead to a lawsuit after the sale.

> **Ask your lawyer.** Should I give buyers a written disclosure, and how should I describe the problems on my list?

---

## 7. If you're married: spousal consent

*If you're married or in a registered domestic partnership, your spouse usually has to consent.*

Under Nova Scotia's *Matrimonial Property Act*, a spouse's consent is needed to sell or mortgage the matrimonial home, even if only one spouse is on the deed. This applies to married couples and registered domestic partners; it doesn't apply to common-law partners who haven't registered.

Your lawyer will arrange the consent. Tell them early if you're married, separated or the home is in one name only.

---

## 8. Offers and deed transfer tax

*Every offer goes to your lawyer before you respond.*

The agreement of purchase and sale sets out the price, deposit, included items, conditions and the closing date. Common conditions in Nova Scotia are financing, a home inspection and, for rural homes, water and septic tests. If your property needs converting, build in the extra time.

- You can accept, counter or decline.
- Once both sides sign and the buyer's conditions are met or removed, the sale is firm.
- Nova Scotia doesn't have a BC-style 3-day buyer cancellation right for resale homes, so get legal advice before you sign.

**Deed transfer tax (paid by the buyer).** Each municipality sets its own rate, from **1% to 1.5%** of the price; Halifax Regional Municipality charges 1.5%. Buyers who don't live in Nova Scotia generally also pay the province's **10% non-resident deed transfer tax** on homes with up to three units, unless they become residents within the time allowed. If you're selling to an out-of-province buyer, expect their lawyer to raise it.

> **Warning: deposits.** The buyer's deposit goes into a lawyer's (or brokerage's) trust account, as the agreement says. Never accept a deposit yourself, by cash, cheque or e-Transfer.

---

## 9. Closing in Nova Scotia

*Your lawyer finishes the sale; you hand over the keys.*

- A few days before closing, you sign the deed, spousal consent if needed and closing papers at your lawyer's office (bring government photo ID).
- On closing day, the lawyers exchange documents and money, the deed is registered, and your lawyer pays off your mortgage.
- Hand over keys, garage remotes and codes as the agreement says. Leave the included items and a clean home, and note the oil or propane level if it's being adjusted.
- Your lawyer sends you the net proceeds and a final report.
- At tax time, report the sale on Schedule 3 and designate the home as your principal residence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I sell my house privately in Nova Scotia?**
Yes. Real estate licensing covers people who act for others in a sale. You'll still need a Nova Scotia lawyer for the agreement, the title and closing.

**How much are closing costs in Nova Scotia?**
For the buyer, the main one is the municipal deed transfer tax of 1% to 1.5% (1.5% in Halifax), plus legal fees, and 10% more if they don't live in Nova Scotia. For you as the seller: your lawyer's fee, any conversion work, your mortgage discharge and any prepayment penalty.

**Do I have to give a Property Disclosure Statement in Nova Scotia?**
No, it isn't required, but most sellers give one and buyers expect it. It records what you told the buyer about the home. Don't hide known problems either way.

**What is migration or conversion of a property in Nova Scotia?**
It's moving your property from the old deed registry into the land registration system. It's required when the property is sold, if it hasn't been done already. Your lawyer handles it; allow a week or two of extra time.

**Will my buyer's property taxes be the same as mine?**
Not necessarily. If your assessment is capped under PVSC's Capped Assessment Program, the cap is removed the year after a sale (unless the buyer is family), so the buyer's taxable assessment, and taxes, may be higher.
