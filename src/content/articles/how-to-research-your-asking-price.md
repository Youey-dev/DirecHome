# How to research your asking price when you sell privately

**Short answer:** start with recent sales of homes like yours, close by, then look at what's for sale now and how long it's been listed. Use your property assessment as a reference, not a price, because it reflects an earlier date. If you want an independent professional opinion, hire a certified appraiser. The price you choose is always your decision.

> **We don't price homes.** OwnersMarket is an advertising platform. This guide explains the method people use to research a price; it doesn't suggest what your home is worth.

---

## 1. Know what your assessment is (and isn't)

*An assessment is for property tax, set as of an earlier date.*

Every province assesses homes for property tax, and your assessment is a free, official starting point. But it's not a sale price:

- It's based on a **valuation date** that's usually months or years before today.
- It's done by **mass appraisal**: assessors analyze many sales and apply typical values to every home. As the City of Calgary puts it, one sale doesn't make a market.
- It doesn't see your renovations, your view, or the damp corner in the basement.

| Province | Who assesses | Valuation date to keep in mind |
|---|---|---|
| British Columbia | BC Assessment | July 1 of the previous year |
| Alberta | Your municipality | July 1 of the previous year (e.g. Calgary) |
| Saskatchewan | SAMA (most municipalities) | 2025 revaluation uses a 2023 base date |
| Ontario | MPAC | January 1, 2016 values (reassessment postponed) |
| Nova Scotia | PVSC | Updated each year; capped taxable values can be far below market |
| New Brunswick | Service New Brunswick | 2026 taxable values frozen at 2025 levels |
| PEI | Province | Owner-occupied values limited to inflation while you own |
| Newfoundland and Labrador | Municipal Assessment Agency; St. John's separately | January 1 values; St. John's every two years |

The older the date, and the more the market has moved since, the less your assessment tells you.

## 2. Find comparable sales

*The most useful information: what similar homes actually sold for, recently.*

A comparable sale (a "comp") is a home that sold recently and is as similar to yours as possible. Appraisers usually look at 3 to 10 or more. Look for:

- **Same type:** detached with detached, townhouse with townhouse, condo with condo
- **Same area:** the same neighbourhood, or as close as you can get; ideally the same school catchment
- **Similar size:** living area, bedrooms and bathrooms, and lot size
- **Similar age and condition:** an updated kitchen or a new roof matters
- **Recent:** the last 3 to 6 months is best; go back further only if there are very few sales

**Where to find sold prices.** It varies by province. Some assessment authorities show a property's sales history; some sold-price websites publish recent sales in certain provinces; land registry searches show registered prices for a fee; and friends or neighbours who sold recently may tell you. In small towns, comparable sales can be few, which makes the next steps more important.

---

## 3. Adjust for the differences

*No two homes are identical. Be honest about yours.*

For each comparable, ask: is my home **better or worse** on this point?

- **Better:** bigger lot, extra bathroom, finished basement, newer roof or furnace, garage, quieter street
- **Worse:** needs updating, smaller, backs onto a busy road, no parking, known issues

Don't put a dollar figure on everything. A simple way: list your comparables from lowest to highest price, then place your home where it honestly fits between them. That gives you a **range**, not a single number.

---

## 4. Look at what's for sale now

*Active listings are your competition.*

- **Similar homes listed now** show what buyers are choosing between today.
- **How long they've been listed.** Homes sitting for weeks are often priced above what buyers will pay. Homes that disappear quickly were often priced close to the market.
- **Price changes.** Repeated reductions tell you where the market didn't meet the asking price.

Asking prices aren't sale prices, so treat listings as context and sold prices as evidence.

---

## 5. Think like a buyer searching online

*Buyers search in price bands.*

Most buyers filter their search with a minimum and maximum price, often in round steps like $400,000 or $500,000. A home listed at $505,000 doesn't show up for someone searching "up to $500,000". It's worth knowing which band your range falls in, and which buyers you'd reach or miss at each end.

---

## 6. Consider a professional appraisal

*If you want an independent opinion, or your home is hard to compare.*

A certified residential appraiser gives an independent, written estimate of market value. They inspect the home and analyze comparable sales, adjusting for differences in location, size, features and condition.

- In Canada, look for the **AACI** or **CRA** designation from the Appraisal Institute of Canada (CRA covers homes with up to four units). The Institute has a "Find an appraiser" tool.
- An appraisal is especially useful for unusual homes (acreages, heritage homes, very high-end or very unique properties) or when there are few recent sales.
- Ask for a fee quote, and ask about their experience with homes like yours.

The buyer's lender may also order its own appraisal as a condition of the buyer's mortgage. If it comes in below the agreed price, the buyer may need to renegotiate or add money; your lawyer will explain your options.

---

## 7. Set your price, then check in after a few weeks

*The market tells you quickly.*

Once you've chosen a price from your research:

- **Watch the first two or three weeks.** Viewing requests and saves are the market's first answer.
- **Few or no viewing requests:** look again at recent sales, your first photo and your description (see [How to list your house for sale by owner](/guides/how-to-list-your-house-for-sale-by-owner)).
- **Plenty of viewings, no offers:** buyers are interested in the home but not at that price, or something seen in person isn't matching the listing.
- **Keep researching:** new sales nearby since you listed are the best update.

> **Ask your lawyer.** Before you list, ask whether anything about your title, zoning or a known issue could affect what buyers will pay, so it's reflected in your research and your listing.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I price my house if I'm selling it myself?**
Research recent sales of similar homes nearby, place your home honestly between them, check what's for sale now, and use your assessment only as a reference. For an independent opinion, hire a certified appraiser. The price is your decision.

**Is my property assessment what my home is worth?**
Not exactly. Assessments are for property tax, use mass appraisal and reflect an earlier valuation date, often months or years ago. They're a useful reference, but recent comparable sales are a better guide.

**Should I get an appraisal before selling my house?**
It's optional. It's most useful for unusual homes, areas with few recent sales, or if you want an independent opinion. Look for an AACI or CRA designation from the Appraisal Institute of Canada.

**How do I find sold prices in my area?**
It varies by province: some assessment authorities show a property's sales history, some websites publish sold prices in certain provinces, and land registry searches show registered prices for a fee. Neighbours who've sold recently can also be a good source.

**What if the buyer's appraisal comes in low?**
If the buyer's lender values the home below the agreed price, the buyer may need to renegotiate, add more money or walk away if their financing condition allows it. Talk to your lawyer about your options before agreeing to any change.
